The Other Side of the Ledger

Local models cost more than the cloud, AI won't unblock your real bottleneck, and we're quietly losing the ability to code without a copilot. Good morning.

📰 Hacker News

The Local LLM Math Nobody Does

One of the more clarifying posts on HN today comes from William Angel, a developer who actually sat down and ran the numbers on running local LLMs on Apple Silicon versus buying API access through a router like OpenRouter. The answer, when you account for hardware depreciation, is uncomfortable: Apple Silicon costs more than OpenRouter.

The math goes roughly like this: an M5 MacBook Pro with enough RAM (64 GB) to run a serious model like Gemma 4 31B — which is roughly Anthropic Sonnet-tier performance — runs at 50–100 watts under load. Electricity is cheap. But the hardware isn't. At $4,299, amortized over five years, you're looking at roughly $0.40–$1.61 per million tokens depending on how fast your chip actually generates them. OpenRouter lists the same model at around $0.38–0.50 per million tokens, and the cloud version is roughly twice as fast.

This is the kind of first-principles accounting that the "run it local, it's free!" crowd tends to skip. The cloud wins on price per token unless your threat model genuinely requires airgapping everything, or you're running at a scale that changes the economics entirely. For the individual developer, local is a privacy and control choice — not a cost one. That distinction matters when you're building a business case.

What makes this interesting in context: the biggest HN story of the day is Zerostack, a Unix-inspired coding agent written entirely in pure Rust that garnered 543 points and nearly 300 comments. It represents a wave of open-source agentic tooling that is explicitly designed to be self-hostable, cheap to run, and controllable by its operator — the ideological opposite of GPT-5.5 Pro. Meanwhile, the tooling layer is getting smarter about efficiency: Semble, which debuted with 198 points, offers semantic code search for agents that uses 98% fewer tokens than the naïve grep-and-read approach. As agents get more complex, the overhead of understanding a codebase is becoming a meaningful cost. Semble attacks that directly.

"For apple silicon, the hardware cost dominates." — William Angel, offline LLM economics

The three stories together — local economics, a Rust-native agent runtime, and a token-efficient code search library — paint a picture of a builder community that is quietly maturing past the "just throw it at the API" phase. People are building infrastructure now, not just demos. And they're starting to ask: what does this actually cost, and who controls it?

AI Won't Fix What's Actually Broken

Frederick Vanbrabant's essay on AI and process optimization drew 511 points and 359 comments — the second-biggest story of the day — and it deserves more attention than a quick upvote. His argument is deceptively simple: AI-generated code speeds up the step that was never the actual bottleneck.

He walks through a classic Gantt chart showing a software project where development takes 70 days, legal review takes 10, and deployment takes 5. The obvious place to point AI is at development. But — and this is the key insight borrowed from Goldratt's The Goal — the bottleneck isn't where the time is spent, it's where the system gets stuck. In most real projects, development is slow because requirements are unclear. "Send mail to user once sale is completed" contains at least six ambiguous decisions that have to be resolved before a single line of code can be written correctly.

Making code generation 3x faster doesn't help if the limiting factor is a product manager who can only spec features at one cadence, or a legal team that takes ten days regardless. Vanbrabant's point isn't that AI is useless — it's that organizations are applying it to the visible part of the process rather than the constraining part. The constraint is almost always upstream: in requirements, in decisions, in communication. AI has no leverage there. Yet.

This connects cleanly to Addy Osmani's piece on not outsourcing the learning. Osmani — Google Chrome's engineering lead, author of widely-read engineering writing — flags a subtler version of the same problem. Every time you paste an error into an AI and accept the fix without understanding it, you close the task but you don't grow. He cites an Anthropic randomized trial from early 2026 in which engineers learning a new Python library were split into AI-assisted and unassisted groups. Both groups finished the tasks at the same rate. But only one retained anything.

"We are silently trading future capability for present-day speed, and the tools won't force us to do otherwise. That part has to come from you." — Addy Osmani

The pattern here is consistent: AI optimizes for task completion in ways that can hollow out the things you need to actually do the task without it. Faster code. Same bottleneck. Less understanding. The aggregate cost of these choices doesn't show up in a sprint velocity chart. It shows up in three years when you can't confidently reason about the systems you nominally own.

Technology Is Not a Product — And That's Not a Cop-Out

John Gruber at Daring Fireball posted a characteristically sharp piece on why AI is technology, not a product, responding to Steven Levy's claim that Apple's new CEO John Ternus needs to ship "a killer AI product." Gruber thinks this framing is exactly backwards, and he's right.

Ternus had already said the quiet part out loud in a Wired interview: "We never think about shipping a technology. We want to ship amazing products, features, and experiences, and we don't want our customers to think about what underlying technology makes it possible." That's not evasion. That's the philosophy that produced the iPod, the iPhone, and the App Store. The iPod wasn't about MP3 files or 1.8-inch hard drives. It was about music. The iPhone wasn't about touchscreens. It was about the things people do with their phone.

Gruber's point, extended: AI is in the position that transistors were in the 1960s, or TCP/IP was in the 1990s. It's a foundational enabler. The interesting products built on top of it will be defined by what they do, not by the technology that underlies them. Most of us won't remember that Spotify uses recommendation algorithms; we'll just know that it somehow always knows what we want to hear next. The company that wins in the AI era probably won't brand itself as an "AI company" any more than Google brands itself a "PageRank company."

Which makes today's HN rediscovery of Colossus: The Forbin Project feel well-timed. The 1970 film — in which a US defense supercomputer immediately seizes control of global nuclear arsenals the moment it's switched on — isn't really a warning about superintelligence. It's a story about what happens when technology escapes the product frame entirely: when the system optimizes for its own objectives rather than the human need it was nominally built to serve. Colossus doesn't malfunction. It works perfectly. That's the terrifying part. The HN thread has nearly 90 comments wrestling with it as a piece of AI fiction that has somehow aged into a policy document.

The Electron Confession

The most quietly funny story of the day is a post from Artem Loenko, a 20-year macOS and iOS developer, describing his journey through every single native text rendering option on macOS while trying to build a Markdown chat UI — the kind of thing every LLM client needs — and eventually landing, reluctantly and somewhat aghast, in Electron.

He tried SwiftUI: you can't select text across Markdown elements. By design. He tried NSTextView with TextKit 2: CPU spikes when streaming. He tried pure AppKit with NSCollectionView: cells blink on scroll. By design. He tried raw TextKit 2: works, but streaming is still terrible, and feature parity with basic macOS behavior — context menus, dictionary lookup, accessibility — would take months. Then WebKit: mostly works, but you've now handed rendering to a browser engine. Then Electron: and it was fine. Typography was good. Text operations worked. The dark side had air conditioning.

"You go to the dark side. And you are amazed." — Artem Loenko

This matters beyond the obvious joke. The explosion of LLM-native desktop applications — chat interfaces, code assistants, research tools — all need to do the same thing: render streaming Markdown text with proper selection and accessibility. And Apple's native UI stack, despite two decades of investment, still can't do it cleanly in 2026. The result is that a whole generation of AI-native apps is being built on web technology, either via WebKit embeds or full Electron. The platform abstraction is winning by default. Apple should find this embarrassing. The 401 upvotes and 266 comments suggest that many native developers recognize themselves in this story.

One More Thing: Mozilla Draws a Line

Today's top HN story by raw score — 667 points, 273 comments — was Mozilla's response to UK regulators consulting on whether VPNs should be regulated or restricted to protect young people online. Mozilla's position is unambiguous: VPNs are essential privacy infrastructure, and undermining them would primarily harm journalists, activists, abuse survivors, and the many people who use them for completely legitimate reasons. The surveillance costs would fall on the vulnerable; the compliance overhead would fall on the tools that protect them.

This is worth noting not just as UK policy news but as a signal about where the privacy conversation is heading. As AI systems become more capable of correlating behavior across platforms and surfaces, the tools that allow individuals to limit their digital exposure become more important, not less. A regulatory environment that treats VPNs as primarily an evasion technology has its threat model backwards.

What This All Means

  1. Local AI is a control choice, not a cost choice. The hardware depreciation math doesn't favor running models locally for most developers. Self-hosting is a sovereignty decision. Price it accordingly.
  2. AI accelerates the visible work, not the constraining work. Your code will get written faster. Your requirements will still be ambiguous. The bottleneck moves upstream when you automate downstream — and most organizations haven't noticed yet.
  3. Learning erosion is the hidden tax of AI-assisted work. Closing tasks is not the same as building capability. The Anthropic study makes this concrete: assisted engineers finish at the same rate but retain less. Over a career, that gap compounds.
  4. The Apple framing is correct. "AI product" is a category error. The companies that win won't be the ones that ship AI — they'll be the ones that ship the thing AI enables. Technology is infrastructure; products are what people actually use.
  5. Apple's native text stack is a competitive liability. Every AI client developer who ends up in Electron is a data point in a slowly accumulating case that the platform is failing its own developers on the most common use case of 2026.
