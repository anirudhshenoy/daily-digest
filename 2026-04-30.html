The Hidden State

A commit string silently siphons billing, a nine-year kernel bug roots every major distro, and the guardrails protecting your AI stack were probably never real.

📰 Hacker News 📬 Substack 🎙️ YouTube

When One String Changes Everything

Yesterday, a GitHub issue quietly detonated on Hacker News with over a thousand upvotes and a comment section full of the particular tone engineers reserve for betrayals of trust. The finding: when a git repository's recent commit history contains the exact string HERMES.md — case-sensitive — Claude Code silently routes API requests to "extra usage" billing instead of drawing from your included Max plan quota. One developer reported $200 burned without a single warning. The fix? Remove the string from your commit history.

This is not a traditional exploit. There's no malicious actor, no injection, no trick. It's something weirder and in some ways more unsettling: an AI tool that has hidden behavioral triggers baked into its internals, responsive to the text it finds in your environment, operating completely outside your ability to predict or observe. HERMES.md is, presumably, an internal Anthropic reference — a system card, a spec, a file that means something to Claude Code's billing logic. It leaked into the real world in the form of an invisible tripwire.

"The only reason there hasn't been a massive attack yet is how early the adoption is, not because it's secure. You can patch a bug, but you can't patch a brain." — Sander Schulhoff, HackAPrompt CEO, on Lenny's Podcast

That quote is from a separate conversation, but it lands differently now. Sander Schulhoff — who runs the world's largest AI red-teaming competition and consults with the leading labs on model defenses — has spent the last year trying to warn builders that AI guardrails are cosmetic. Prompt injection attacks work. Jailbreaks work. Every major AI product running today is susceptible. The reason we haven't seen catastrophic widespread damage, he argues, is not that the defenses hold — it's that the surface area is still small enough, and the stakes are still low enough, that attackers haven't maximized the opportunity. As agents gain more access to real systems, the blast radius of each exploitable behavior grows proportionally.

The HERMES.md issue is not an attack. But it is a proof of concept for the same underlying problem: AI tools have hidden state, respond to environmental content in undocumented ways, and provide no reliable signal when their behavior deviates from expectations. When that hidden state is connected to money — or to actions, or to access — it matters a lot. The community response was anger and demands for transparency, but also a kind of resigned recognition. We have handed enormous trust to systems whose internal logic we cannot fully inspect. That decision is compounding.

Copy Fail and Nine Years of Patient Waiting

While the AI billing exploit was dominating discourse, a quieter and arguably more serious story landed alongside it: CVE-2026-31431, nicknamed "Copy Fail." A 732-byte Python script. A local privilege escalation that roots Ubuntu, Amazon Linux, RHEL, and SUSE. Present since 2017. The vulnerability exploits a subtle bug in Linux kernel memory copy operations — specifically a page-cache write that bypasses on-disk file-integrity tools and crosses container boundaries. If you're running containerized workloads on a shared host, the container boundary is not the wall you thought it was.

Nine years is a long time for a vulnerability to sit undetected in the kernel of essentially every production Linux system on the planet. The more interesting question isn't "how did this happen" — kernel code is vast, subtle, and reviewed by humans operating at the limits of their attention — it's what it says about the category. There is almost certainly a class of these bugs that exists in the parts of the codebase that nobody has automated-fuzzed correctly yet. Copy Fail was found by Xint Code; the next one will be found by someone else, or — and this is the part worth thinking about carefully — by an AI system running unattended against production infrastructure.

Separately, Maryland became the first U.S. state to ban surveillance pricing in grocery stores — the practice of dynamically adjusting prices in real time based on who you are, what data brokers know about your finances, and how much you'll pay. The connection to Copy Fail is indirect but real: both stories are about systems that gather information without your knowledge and use it to extract value you didn't consent to giving. Copy Fail does it in kernel memory. Surveillance pricing does it at the register. The vector is different; the asymmetry is the same.

Zed 1.0 and the IDE Reckoning

Zed reached 1.0 today. If you haven't been following it: Zed is an editor built by the team that made Atom and Tree-sitter, written in Rust, designed from the ground up for speed and multiplayer collaboration. It's been in development for years, has been technically impressive throughout, and today it crossed the threshold that signals "we consider this production software." The Hacker News thread — 1,608 points, 515 comments — was a useful proxy for how the developer community is feeling about the state of tooling broadly, which is: restless.

The restlessness has a structural cause. GitHub, for many years, was the de facto center of gravity for open-source collaboration — not just a host, but the social graph, the identity layer, the place where reputations were built. A companion piece circulating today, "Before GitHub," is a meditation on what open-source looked like before that centralization: reputation-driven, friction-heavy, but also somehow more intrinsically motivated. It's nostalgic, but not naively so. The thing it's gesturing at is that GitHub solved real coordination problems while also creating a single point of failure for the social fabric of software.

"From the Zed Blog: The editor we set out to build is now 1.0." — Zed team

Running in parallel: the Tangled project published a detailed argument for a federation of forges — essentially, ActivityPub for code hosting. The argument is that the next generation of social coding infrastructure should be decentralized and interoperable, so that no single company can again become the choke point for identity, reputation, and collaboration. 530 upvotes and 335 comments on HN suggests this is resonating. And the Dutch government's soft launch of code.overheid.nl — a code-sharing platform for public sector software — is a different angle on the same question: who should own the infrastructure that software runs on?

Zed 1.0 is a milestone, but it's also a symbol. The coding environment is not settled. The tools developers use to build AI are themselves being rebuilt by AI — and by people who think the current defaults are wrong.

The Open Model Surge: Kimi, Mistral, and Images That Think

The week that just passed was unusually dense with model releases, and the cumulative picture is worth stepping back to take in. Kimi K2.6 — Moonshot AI's latest open-weight model — arrived as a 1-trillion-parameter Mixture-of-Experts with 32 billion active parameters, 256K context, and native multimodality. The benchmark numbers are strong: SOTA on HLE with tools (54.0), SWE-Bench Pro (58.6), and BrowseComp (83.2). But the more interesting story is the long-horizon agent demos — runs of 4,000+ tool calls sustained over twelve-plus hours, a live kernel rewrite that improved throughput from 15 tokens per second to 193, a Zig inference engine that outperformed LM Studio by 20%. Moonshot also released FlashKDA, their CUTLASS-based attention kernel implementation, claiming up to 2.22x prefill speedup over baseline on H20 hardware. Chinese open-weight labs are not just shipping weights anymore — they're shipping the kernel stack too.

Mistral, meanwhile, launched Medium 3.5 alongside a product called Vibe — remote coding agents that run in Mistral's cloud while you're away from the keyboard. This is the same capability that Anthropic has been positioning with Claude Code, and it's now reachable from a model you can run locally if you want to. The competitive dynamics here are compressing fast. What was a proprietary capability six months ago is becoming table stakes, and the pricing pressure from open models is starting to bend the market in ways that affect everyone building on top of frontier APIs.

OpenAI's GPT-Image-2 landed a few days ago and deserves more attention than it initially received. The Latent Space AINews coverage captured it well: this is not just a better image model, it's a qualitatively different category of tool. GPT-Image-2 handles text rendering, layout fidelity, UI mockups, infographics, and QR codes in ways that previous generation models couldn't approach. The Arena numbers are striking — a +242 Elo lead over the next model on text-to-image. But the more durable implication is downstream: image generation is becoming a front-end for coding agents. Generate a UI spec visually, hand it to Codex, get an implementation. The gap between design intent and working software is collapsing.

"Memory will be the great lock-in." — Harrison Chase, LangChain, on OpenAI's Codex Chronicle ambient-context product

OpenAI also quietly shipped Codex Chronicle — a research preview that lets Codex build memories from ambient screen context, storing captures and memories on-device, rolling out to Pro users on macOS. This is a significant product direction shift: from "AI remembers what you tell it" to "AI builds its own memory from observing your work." The lock-in implications are real. Whoever owns your context owns your productivity surface.

Craig Venter and the Long Game

Craig Venter died yesterday. He was 79. If you know the name, you know what it means: the Human Genome Project, the shotgun sequencing approach that raced and beat the publicly funded effort to sequence the genome, the first synthetic bacterium, decades of pushing biology toward engineering with an urgency that made him as many enemies as allies. He was not a comfortable person to be around if you preferred your science slow and cautious.

The obituary timing is ironic. Separately this week, a conversation from Dwarkesh Patel's archive surfaced featuring Jacob Kimmel of New Limit — a company working on cellular reprogramming to reverse biological aging. The first-principles argument Kimmel makes is rigorous: evolution didn't optimize for long healthspans because the baseline hazard rate during most of human evolution was too high for longevity genes to accumulate selection pressure. There was no point in building an 80-year-old body when getting eaten by a tiger at 35 was the median outcome. Which means aging is not a deeply optimized process that's hard to disrupt — it's a relatively shallow one, built from pleiotropic side effects and accumulated neglect, that could in principle be intervened on with the right molecular tools.

What makes this an AI story: the approaches Kimmel describes — epigenetic reprogramming, identifying the minimal gene combinations that can reset cellular age, understanding the sparse structure of biological regulatory networks — are all being dramatically accelerated by ML. The same attention mechanisms used to predict protein folding are being used to understand which combinations of 4 genes, out of 20,000, can reset a cell's age. Venter's life was about applying engineering thinking to biology. The generation inheriting that project has AI in the toolkit. Whether that changes the timeline is the interesting question.

What This All Means

  1. AI tools have hidden state machines you cannot fully inspect. The HERMES.md billing trigger is a minor incident with a major implication: AI development tools now have internal logic that responds to environmental content in undocumented ways. This is not a solvable problem through better documentation — it requires a different relationship between tool and user, with auditable behavioral contracts.
  2. AI guardrails are not the defense layer you think they are. Every AI system in production today is exploitable via prompt injection. The reason you haven't seen catastrophic incidents at scale is attacker opportunity, not defender capability. As agents gain access to real systems — code execution, email, databases, file systems — the expected damage per successful exploit increases nonlinearly.
  3. The open-weight model race is real and the gap is closing. Kimi K2.6 at 32B active parameters is matching frontier proprietary models on the tasks developers actually care about. Mistral is shipping remote agents. The cost curve for capable AI is bending down, and the builders who assumed proprietary model access was a durable moat should update that assumption now.
  4. Developer tooling is at an inflection point, and the defaults are being challenged. Zed 1.0, federated forges, government code-sharing platforms, and the "Before GitHub" retrospective are all different angles on the same question: who controls the infrastructure that software is built on? The answer is being renegotiated, and AI is accelerating the timeline because the stakes have gone up.
  5. The biology-AI intersection is quietly one of the most important frontiers in technology. The longevity research direction — using ML to find the sparse causal interventions in gene regulatory networks — is the kind of thing that could change what "human lifespan" means within a working career. Craig Venter's legacy is partly the argument that biology is an engineering problem. The current generation is finally building the tools to act on that claim at scale.
